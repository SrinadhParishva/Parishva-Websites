Why Your Brand Attracts the Wrong Customers (And How to Fix It)

Read Time5 min
AuthorParishva Branding Studio
" The customers haggling over your price aren't your customers. They're proof your brand is speaking to the wrong room.

There's a particular kind of tired that comes from getting sales and still feeling like something's wrong. The enquiries come in, but they ask for a discount before they ask a single question about the product. The customers convert, but they email three times a week, argue about things that were clearly stated upfront, and churn the moment a cheaper option shows up. The instinct is to blame the customers — call them "difficult," tighten the refund policy, raise the price and hope it filters them out. Usually, the real issue sits one level upstream. The brand itself is the one inviting them in.

A Brand Is a Filter, Whether You Design It to Be One or Not

Every brand sends signals before a single sale happens — the words on the homepage, the tone of the captions, the way the pricing is framed, the channels the content shows up on. People read those signals and self-select. Some see themselves in it and lean in. Others don't, and scroll past. That filtering happens whether you've thought about it or not — the only choice a brand actually has is whether the filter is deliberate or accidental.

Here's the part most businesses miss: an accidental filter still filters — it just pulls in whoever those unintended signals happen to appeal to, which is very often not the customer the product was actually built for. A premium product marketed with discount-driven language will get bargain hunters. A considered, slow-made brand marketed on high-volume, low-context platforms will get people who never wanted "considered" in the first place. The product hasn't changed. The filter has quietly decided who walks through the door.

A founder selling a premium, small-batch home fragrance line kept getting buyers who complained the price was "too high for a candle." The product was never overpriced for its actual audience — but every Instagram Reel used trending discount-sale audio, every caption led with "35% off," and the brand's only visible presence was a marketplace storefront sitting next to mass-produced competitors. The brand wasn't attracting the wrong customers by accident. It was speaking fluent bargain-hunter and wondering why bargain hunters kept showing up.

The Five Signals That Quietly Pull In the Wrong Crowd

Wrong-fit customers rarely arrive because of one dramatic misstep. They arrive because a handful of ordinary signals, stacked together, tell a story the brand didn't mean to tell. Below are the five that show up most often, ranked by how strongly each one typically skews the customer mix.

01Discount-Led Positioning
High pull

Leading every post, email, and landing page with a sale, a coupon, or "limited time" urgency. It works — but it trains the audience to evaluate the brand on price before anything else, and price-trained audiences rarely become loyal, full-price customers later.

Wrong-fit pullHigh
02Messaging Built to Exclude No One
High pull

Copy written to appeal to "everyone who might need this," out of fear of narrowing the audience too much. Broad messaging doesn't attract more of the right people — it attracts a wider spread of people, most of whom were never a strong fit to begin with.

Wrong-fit pullHigh
03Visual Identity Priced Differently Than the Product
High pull

A budget-looking identity around a premium product invites budget shoppers who then balk at the price. A luxury-looking identity around an accessible product quietly scares off the everyday buyers it was actually built for. Either mismatch pulls in the wrong side of the market.

Wrong-fit pullHigh
04A Voice Borrowed From a Different Category
Medium pull

Copying the tone of a trending brand in an entirely different price tier or values set — playful and irreverent for a category that's actually chosen for trust and expertise, or clinical and formal for a category people buy on emotion. The borrowed voice recruits the wrong crowd for the actual offer.

Wrong-fit pullMedium
05Chasing Volume Channels Over Fit Channels
Medium pull

Going wherever the algorithm sends the most eyeballs, rather than where the actual ideal customer spends time. A channel that reaches ten times more people but shares almost nothing with the target audience just delivers ten times more mismatch, faster.

The Product Isn't the Fix — the Filter Is

It's tempting, once wrong-fit customers pile up, to start adjusting the product itself — softening the price, adding features to satisfy complaints that were never going to be satisfied, chasing feedback from people who were never the intended buyer. That usually makes things worse, because it further blurs a filter that was already sending mixed signals. A handful of things actually recalibrate who a brand attracts, and none of them start with the product:

  • A defined ideal customer. Not a demographic — a set of values, priorities, and decision criteria specific enough that most people would recognise themselves in it, or clearly not.
  • Pricing signals that match the identity. Discounts, bundles, and urgency language attract deal-seekers; clear, confident, non-negotiated pricing attracts people who value what's being sold.
  • A voice that repels as much as it invites. A message that appeals to absolutely everyone appeals strongly to no one — the right tone should make the wrong-fit customer feel, correctly, that this isn't for them.
  • Channels chosen for overlap, not size. The best channel is where the ideal customer already is, even if it's smaller than the one with the biggest reach.

How to Recalibrate Who You Attract

None of this requires starting over. It requires being honest about which of the five signals above the brand is currently sending, and adjusting them on purpose:

  • Write down who the last ten "wrong-fit" customers actually were, and what they had in common — that pattern usually points straight at the signal attracting them.
  • Audit messaging for what it's currently optimised to do: sound appealing to the most people, or sound unmistakably right to the fewest, best-fit people.
  • Check whether pricing is presented as a negotiation (discounts, "best price," haggling room) or a statement (clear, confident, non-negotiable) — the framing itself is a filter.
  • List every channel currently in use and rate each one on actual overlap with the ideal customer, not follower count or reach.
Attracting more customers isn't the same as attracting the right ones — and a brand that keeps optimising for volume over fit will keep solving the wrong problem, one mismatched sale at a time.

What This Means in Practice

If the customers showing up keep haggling, complaining, or churning, resist the pull to keep adjusting the product or the price to accommodate them. They may simply be responding correctly to signals the brand is sending — just not the signals it meant to send. Recalibrate the filter — the positioning, the pricing framing, the voice, the channels — and the right customers, the ones who were never going to argue about the price in the first place, finally get a clear invitation to show up.

Your brand isn't underperforming. It's just answering a question your actual customer never asked.
