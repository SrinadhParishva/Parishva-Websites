Market Positioning: The Space You Own in Someone Else's Mind

PublishedSeptember 21, 2026
CategoryBrand Strategy
Read Time6 min read
AuthorParishva Studio
" Positioning isn't a claim you make. It's the one thought a prospect has about you before they think of anyone else.

Ask most business owners where they sit in their market and they'll describe a list of services, a set of values, or a tone they're aiming for. Ask them to name the one space they occupy that no one else does, and the answer usually gets vague. That vagueness is not a communication problem — it's a positioning problem. Market positioning isn't the sum of what you offer or how nicely you say it. It's the specific, defensible spot you hold in a prospect's mind relative to every alternative they could choose instead. Most brands never choose that spot deliberately. They let the market assign it to them by default — and the default is rarely flattering.

Positioning Is Not the Same as Differentiation

These two words get used interchangeably, and the confusion costs businesses real growth. Differentiation is what makes you different — a faster process, a specific specialism, a distinctive point of view. Positioning is where that difference actually lives: the place it occupies in the mind of the person deciding who to hire, relative to the other options they're weighing.

You can have genuine differentiation and still have no position at all, because nobody has been given a reason to file you under anything specific. A studio might genuinely work faster, think more strategically, or design more distinctively than its competitors — and still get compared on price, because price is the only axis its marketing ever gave the market to compare it on.

Differentiation is a fact about your business. Positioning is a decision about how that fact gets planted in someone else's head — and defended once it's there.

Two branding studios, similar quality of work. The first describes itself as doing "everything from strategy to web to social, for businesses of every size." The second says it "builds brand systems exclusively for premium hospitality brands." The first gets more enquiries and fewer serious ones. The second gets fewer enquiries — and wins the larger, better-fit clients almost every time, because it's the only name that comes to mind for that specific problem.

If You Don't Position Yourself, the Market Will Do It For You

Positioning isn't optional. Every business occupies some position in the minds of the people who encounter it — the only question is who chose it. If you haven't deliberately claimed a space, prospects will assign you one anyway, usually by comparing you to whichever competitor is easiest to reference. Left undefined, that default position is almost always worse than the one you'd have chosen: "the cheaper version of the studio everyone already knows," or "another generalist, like all the others."

Once a position has been assigned — by the market, by a competitor's louder claim, or simply by silence — it's expensive to change. People don't re-evaluate impressions casually. The business that waits to "figure out its positioning later" is really choosing to let someone else figure it out first.

The Positioning Spectrum

Positioning shows up along a handful of recurring spectrums — pairs of opposite claims a brand could make. A brand that hasn't chosen a position tends to sit near the middle of every one of them, trying to appeal a little to everyone. A brand with real positioning sits clearly toward one end, on purpose, and defends it.

The Middle Is the Most Dangerous Place to Stand

The shaded band running through the centre of each spectrum above is where most undifferentiated brands actually sit — not because they chose it, but because trying to appeal broadly pulls a brand toward the average of every claim at once. Not too premium, not too cheap. Not too specialised, not too general. Safe on every axis, and therefore invisible on all of them.

The middle feels safer because it excludes no one. In practice it compels no one either. A prospect scanning a crowded market isn't looking for the option that offends nobody — they're looking for the option that was clearly built for someone like them. A brand standing at the edge of a spectrum gets remembered, argued for, and recommended by name. A brand standing in the middle gets compared on whichever variable is left: usually price.

  • The category trap. If you don't name the specific category you compete in, prospects will slot you into the nearest generic one — and compare you against everyone in it, fairly or not.
  • The "for everyone" fallacy. A brand that's "for every business" is, in the mind of any specific business, for none of them in particular. Precision about who it's for is what makes someone feel it's for them.
  • Proof of position. A claimed position only holds if the evidence backs it up — case studies, pricing, tone, and client mix all need to agree. A position stated once in an "about" page and contradicted everywhere else isn't a position, it's a slogan.
  • The cost of repositioning. Like a first price anchor, a first impression of your category is hard to move. Choosing well early is far cheaper than correcting course after the market has already filed you somewhere.

How to Find and Hold Your Position

Positioning isn't invented from nothing, and it isn't copied from a competitor's language either. It's built from a small number of deliberate choices, made once and then defended everywhere.

Start by naming the category precisely — not "branding" but the specific version of branding you practice, for the specific kind of client you do it best for. Then identify, with equal precision, who it's explicitly not for; excluding the wrong prospects is what makes the right ones feel seen. Choose one attribute you're willing to be known for above all others, and resist the temptation to also claim the attributes your competitors already own. Then prove that one claim relentlessly, in the same language, across every case study, every price point, and every conversation — because a position repeated inconsistently is not a position, it's noise.

The question is never "what could we also offer?" It's "what's the one thing we want to be the obvious answer to?" Everything else in the brand should be built to support that answer, not compete with it.

What This Means in Practice

If you're reviewing your own positioning, the diagnostic questions are simple to ask and uncomfortable to answer honestly.

  • If a client described your business in one sentence, would it sound like your positioning — or like a generic description of your industry?
  • Is there a specific competitor people default to comparing you against? What position do they hold that you haven't claimed?
  • Looking at the Positioning Spectrum, are you sitting confidently toward one end of each axis — or clustered in the blur zone, trying to be reasonable about everything?
  • Would your positioning statement survive being said out loud to a prospect, or does it only work as copy on a website nobody reads twice?

Positioning is not a paragraph in your brand guidelines. It's the sentence that runs through someone's head the moment your name comes up. Choose it before the market chooses it for you.

You don't need to be right for everyone. You need to be unmistakable for someone.
